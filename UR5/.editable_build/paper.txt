1. Introduction

    In the era of intelligent manufacturing, there is a high demand for automated welding of complex curved structural components, as
well as high requirements for welding precision, welding quality, and adaptability to non-structured environments. Robots are hailed
as the jewel in the crown of manufacturing and the physical execution terminal of intelligent manufacturing. They play an important
role in improving industrial production eﬃciency, realizing ﬂexible manufacturing, and performing high-risk, high-precision welding
operations [1–4]. Traditional 6-degree-of-freedom welding robots have limited working spaces and insuﬃcient ﬂexibility. When faced
with complex constraints such as conﬁned spaces, singular points, and obstructions caused by the workpiece itself, they struggle to
generate high-quality welding trajectories. Redundant welding robotic arms are an ideal solution to this challenge. Their additional

degrees of freedom enable them to optimize a series of secondary tasks (such as obstacle avoidance, singular point avoidance, and
joint limit avoidance) while fulﬁlling the primary task of controlling the position and orientation of the welding gun. In the event of a
joint failure, the redundant robotic arm can redistribute speed and torque to the aﬀected joint and continue to execute the established
operational tasks using the remaining joints, thereby oﬀering enhanced ﬂexibility and fault tolerance.
    Redundant robotic arms provide ﬂexibility for welding operations while also posing signiﬁcant challenges for motion planning
algorithms, with the core challenge being the solution of the inverse kinematics problem. In real-world welding scenarios, this is not
merely an IK solution problem but a multi-objective, strongly coupled, high-dimensional real-time optimization problem that must
satisfy various complex constraints. For example, task space constraints require the welding gun tip to precisely track the predeﬁned
trajectory and posture. Joint space constraints require that the joint angles, velocities, and accelerations of the robotic arm do not
exceed speciﬁed limits. Obstacle avoidance constraints require that the robotic arm does not collide with the welded part or any
obstacles in the environment. How to eﬃciently and stably solve the inverse kinematics problem of a redundant robotic arm that
incorporates all these complex constraints within a uniﬁed framework is a highly challenging open problem in the ﬁeld of robotics
[5].
    Presently, methods for solving the inverse kinematics of redundant robotic arms can be classiﬁed into four main categories:
kinematic transformation methods, numerical optimization methods, heuristic algorithms, and learning-based methods. Kinematic
transformation methods can be further divided into analytical, geometric, and arm-angle methods. The analytical method yields
the exact inverse solution through formula derivation; however, the applicable conﬁgurations of robotic arms are limited, thereby
restricting the ﬂexibility of this approach [6,7]. In fact, the majority of redundant robotic arms lack analytical solutions. Similarly,
the geometric method is contingent upon the arm conﬁguration design, which precludes a generalized computational approach and
constrains its applicability [8,9]. The arm angle method constrains the conﬁguration by deﬁning a speciﬁc arm angle, ensuring that
the number of joints equals the number of equations to facilitate the determination of the inverse solution [10]. The above kinematic
transformation methods are fast to calculate but diﬃcult to handle inequality constraints.
    Numerical optimization methods include geometric iterative methods, damped least squares methods, mathematical optimization
methods, and their respective enhancements. The stringent constraints and complex structures of cable-driven redundant robotic
arms pose challenges to inverse kinematics calculations. Several researchers have employed multiple geometric iterative methods
to solve inverse kinematics [11,12], with simulation results demonstrating their eﬀectiveness. The main idea of the damped least
squares method is to introduce a damping factor to achieve a balance between the tracking accuracy of the end-eﬀector trajectory
and the stability of joint motion, thereby avoiding numerical instability caused by the singularity of the Jacobian matrix [13–15]. Its
performance depends on the intelligent adjustment strategy of the damping factor. Inverse kinematics problems can also be modeled as
mathematical optimization problems and addressed using optimization techniques while incorporating relevant constraints to achieve
globally optimal solutions [16,17]. The above numerical optimization methods are more powerful in handling multiple constraints,
but they are computationally expensive and sensitive to initial values and parameters.
    Heuristic algorithms center on converting the inverse kinematics problem of redundant robotic arms into an optimization prob-
lem, employing heuristic algorithms and their associated enhancements for resolution [18–23]. Some scholars have also adopted
multiobjective heuristic methods to solve inverse kinematics problems. Reddy et al. [24] formulated the inverse kinematics problem
as a multiobjective optimization and resolved it using MOPSO. Wu et al. [25] utilized an enhanced NSGA-II algorithm to resolve
the inverse kinematics of an 8-degree-of-freedom redundant robotic arm, ensuring accuracy, continuity, and real-time performance.
The above heuristic algorithms have strong global search abilities, but they are mostly designed for single-objective optimization
problems. For multi-objective optimization algorithms such as MOPSO and NSGA-II, they are diﬃcult to handle complex constraints,
which limits their widespread application.
    Certain literature utilizes learning-based methods such as neural networks and reinforcement learning to tackle the inverse kine-
matics problems of redundant robotic arms. For instance, Dong et al. [26] partitioned the workspace of the robotic arm and employed
a pre-trained neural network model for each partition to achieve high-precision inverse kinematics solutions. Ho et al. [27] modeled
the solution space of the robotic arm with neural networks to compute inverse kinematics, facilitating the assessment of additional
obstacle avoidance constraints during the optimization process. Habibkhah et al. [28] initially introduced a virtual vector function
into the task space, ensuring that the number of variables in the task space is at least equal to that in the joint space. They subsequently
utilized artiﬁcial neural networks to address the inverse kinematics problem. Hlavac et al. [29] derived inverse kinematics solutions
for redundant robotic arms through the training of a multilayer perceptron. Vu et al. [30] proposed a real-time framework for solving
inverse kinematics, utilizing neural networks to learn the redundant parameters of the robotic arm. Simulation results corroborated
the high precision and real-time performance of this framework. Emami et al. [31] conducted a review of multi-agent reinforcement
learning methods applied to solving the inverse kinematics problems of redundant robotic arms. In addition, some researchers have
employed some hybrid methods [32]. These cutting-edge methods are very promising, but they require large amounts of high-quality
data for training, and their generalization capabilities are limited.
    In summary, existing methods either limit their ability to solve for generalization or compromise on solution accuracy. The ability
of these methods to handle multiple complex constraints needs to be further improved. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop
a new paradigm for inverse kinematics optimization that combines generality, optimality in solving, and robust handling of multiple
complex constraints.
    To address the aforementioned challenges, this paper transforms the obstacle avoidance inverse kinematics problem of redun-
dant robotic arms into a constrained multiobjective optimization problem and proposes an auxiliary-based dual-stage constrained
multiobjective evolutionary algorithm, denoted as ADCMEA. The novelty of the proposed ADCMEA in solving inverse kinematics
problems is as follows. First, a global competitive swarm optimizer was developed, where the winner individuals also undergo learn-

    In automobile manufacturing, welding robotic arms have become an important tool for the automation and intelligence of welding
operations, and their level of automation and intelligence directly aﬀects the production eﬃciency, quality, and cost of vehicles.
However, the workshop environment is complex and variable, and there are often various obstacles, such as weldments, conveyors,
unmanned vehicles, wall columns, and pedestrians. The redundant welding robotic arms, due to their redundant degrees of freedom,
can be used to avoid obstacles in the workshop, maintain joint continuity, etc., in addition to accomplishing the main welding task.
This paper considers several common obstacles, including welding tables, weldments, conveyors, and welding workshop columns.

diﬀerent application scenarios, the common types of welding trajectories include straight-line, circular arc, and spiral trajectories. To
realize this goal, the Bezier curve is used to approximate the welding trajectory and generate interpolation points.

like a stretchable leather band. Various smooth curves can be ﬂexibly generated by adjusting the position of the control points. Due
to these characteristics, Bezier curves are widely used in architectural design, vehicle design, industrial engineering, and other ﬁelds.

3. Data-driven prediction of obstacle collisions

3.1. Dataset acquisition

    Each joint of the manipulator arm has its own range of motion limitations, as shown in Table 1. These range combinations
constitute the working space of the robotic arm. The robotic arm is interconnected with each static obstacle in the workspace.
Diﬀerent joint combinations of the robotic arm may or may not encounter obstacles. Most of the existing collision detection methods
use the bounding volume method to wrap the robotic arm and the obstacle with regular geometries and then determine whether the
geometries intersect with each other to detect the collision or directly simplify the robotic arm to perform the computation [34].
Although the bounding volume method is simple and eﬃcient, it also has the disadvantages of insuﬃcient tightness, poor adaptability
to complex objects, high updating cost, and limited collision detection accuracy. The multiobjective optimization method proposed
in this paper needs to update the position of each joint of the robotic arm in real-time, and it will be very time-consuming and limited
in accuracy if the collision detection is carried out by the bounding volume method. Learning the relative relationship between the
robotic arm and obstacles in the workspace can improve eﬃciency. For this reason, this paper proposes a data-driven prediction
method for detection.
    Sampling is ﬁrst performed in the workspace with a sample size of 30,000, representing 30,000 joint combinations. For learn-
ing the features of each region in the workspace, an optimized Latin hypercube sampling method based on the max-min idea
is used [35]. This method overcomes the aggregation phenomena of sample points that may occur in traditional Latin hyper-
cube sampling methods. The core idea is: when selecting each sample point, not only consider its position within the respec-
tive variable interval but also consider the minimum distance between it and the selected sample point and try to maximize
this minimum distance, so that the sample points are more discretely distributed throughout the variable space, thus improv-
ing the spatial ﬁllability. Since the sample points are more evenly distributed, the accuracy and reliability of the model can be
improved.
    In this paper, the method of minimum distance detection is used to determine whether an obstacle is encountered. When the
minimum distance between each link of the robot arm and the obstacles is 0, it means that it touches the obstacle, and greater than
0 means that it does not touch the obstacle. Take link-5 in two joint combinations for instance. The ﬁrst joint combination is [100◦ ,
−20◦ , 70◦ , 50◦ , 120◦ , −90◦ , −75◦ ]. The minimum distances between the link-5 and the weldment, conveyor, workshop column are
obtained through the minimum distance detection algorithm in the Coppeliasim software. They are 0.164 m, 0.168 m, and 0.437 m,
respectively. In this situation, the robotic arm did not touch the obstacles in the environment, as shown in Fig. 4(a). The second
joint combination is [100◦ , −50◦ , 70◦ , 50◦ , 120◦ , −90◦ , −75◦ ]. Similarly, the minimum distances between link-5 and the weldment,
conveyor, and workshop column are 0 m, 0.179 m, and 0.628 m, respectively. In this situation, link-5 collides with the weldment
mutually, as shown in Fig. 4(b). Through the above analysis, we can deﬁne the collision state of the whole robotic arm with the
obstacles in the environment, as shown in Eq. (7).

3.2. Collision prediction for redundant robotic arms

    In this section, we use a feed-forward backpropagation neural network (FFBPNN) to perform the prediction process [36]. The
training process of FFBPNN consists of two phases: forward propagation of information and backward propagation of errors. In the
forward propagation stage, the input data are propagated forward through the layers of the network until the output results are
produced. In the backward propagation phase, the weights of the network are updated according to errors between output results
and desired results to minimize the errors. Due to its powerful nonlinear mapping ability and self-learning and self-adaptive ability,
the BP neural network is widely used in the ﬁelds of function approximation, pattern recognition, classiﬁcation, etc., and is one of
the most widely used neural networks.
    The accuracy of collision prediction will be veriﬁed on four welding paths of two parts. The number of training samples is 30,000,
and the number of additional test samples is 1000. The simulation experiments are performed in MATLAB software. The speciﬁc
parameters are set as follows. The training algorithm is Levenberg-Marquardt. The performance is evaluated by mean square error
(MSE). The learning rate is set to 0.01. The network uses two hidden layers, and the number of neurons in each layer is set to 50.
The activation function uses the sigmoid function. The last layer adopts the purelin function for output. The ﬁtting curves are shown
in Fig. 5; it is clear that the correlation coeﬃcients of the two parts are greater than 0.9, indicating that the training process of the
FFBPNN is excellent.
    Fig. 6(a) and (b) show the error distribution histograms for 1000 additional test samples. As can be seen from the plots, only a
few sample points have large prediction errors, while about 90 % of the sample points have errors within 0.05 m, indicating that the
results are reliable. The MSE is 0.00083 for the straight and polygonal welding scene and 0.001 for the circular and L-shaped welding
scene. These two trained FFBPNN models will be used as constraints in the multiobjective optimization stage to predict the collision
state of the robotic arm to reduce computational cost and complexity. The speciﬁc expressions for the obstacle avoidance constraints
will be given in the next subsection.

4.2. The proposed ADCMEA algorithm

   The constrained multiobjective optimization problem is complex. There are constraints in both the objective space and the decision
space, and the feasible domain is small. Solving the problem is very challenging. Dealing with this type of problem requires addressing
the following three issues simultaneously, i.e., how to better generate the solution, how to better select the solution, and how to
better handle the constraints. Following this idea, we divide the evolutionary process into an objective optimization phase and a
constraint handling phase and propose the population-auxiliary two-stage constrained multiobjective evolutionary algorithm, denoted
as ADCMEA.
   This subsection is divided into four parts for detailed description. Section 4.2.1 presents the general framework of the proposed
ADCMEA. Section 4.2.2 introduces a global competitive swarm optimizer. Section 4.2.3 discusses a new ﬁtness evaluation method in
detail. Finally, the proposed constraint handling technique is presented.

4.2.1. The overall framework of ADCMEA
    The Algorithm 1 gives the overall framework of the ADCMEA. First, the population 𝑃 and the auxiliary population 𝑃1 are randomly
initialized with 𝑁 individuals, respectively. The population evolution is divided into two stages according to the value of 𝑟𝑎. The ﬁrst
stage is the objective optimization stage, and the second stage is the constraint handling stage. In the objective optimization phase, the

optimizer (GCSO) is developed to generate the oﬀspring, as described in Section 4.2.2. In the individual selection phase, a novel

4.2.4. Constraint handling techniques
    The constrained dominance principle (CDP) [41] is widely used because of its simplicity and easy implementation. However,
the CDP method focuses too much on constraint satisfaction and weakens the optimization information of objectives, making the
population easily trapped in the local optimal region. The 𝜖 constraint handling technique dynamically adjusts the value of the 𝜖
parameter to achieve a balance between the objectives and constraints. This ﬂexibility allows it to be applied to a variety of complex
constrained multiobjective optimization problems.
    For the constrained multiobjective optimization problem required to be solved in this paper, a combination of two constraint
handling techniques is adopted to achieve complementary advantages. The main population 𝑃 adopts the CDP constraint handling
technique because the main population has to ensure the strict satisfaction of the constraints, i.e., 𝐸𝑆1 adopts the environment
selection strategy proposed in the literature [42]. The auxiliary population 𝑃1 uses a modiﬁed 𝜖 constraint handling technique [38]
𝐸𝑆2. The constraint violation boundary value 𝐶𝐵 of this technique changes dynamically as iterations, and its dynamic function is
shown in Eq. (16). In the early stage, more objective information can be considered for a larger value of 𝐶𝐵, while in the later stage it
gradually focuses on constraint satisfaction to achieve a dynamic balance between objective optimization and constraint satisfaction.
The environmental selection 𝐸𝑆2 is shown in Algorithm 3. The framework ﬁrst divides the population into feasible population 𝑃𝑓
and infeasible population 𝑃𝑖𝑓 according to the constraint violation boundary value 𝐶𝐵, and then divides the feasible population
𝑃𝑓 into three cases, i.e., |𝑃𝑓 | = 0, |𝑃𝑓 | ≤ 𝑁 and |𝑃𝑓 | > 𝑁. For the ﬁrst case (lines 5–17), i.e., there are all infeasible solutions in
the population, the ﬁtness value is calculated according to the CDP method. It is worth noting that during the process of solution
selection, if the number of individuals is less than 𝑁, the individuals are all selected; for the second case (lines 18–26), i.e., when

Z. He and H. Liu                                                                               Applied Mathematical Modelling 151 (2026) 116440


the number of feasible solutions is less than 𝑁, the feasible solutions are all selected, and then the 𝑁 − |𝑃𝑓 | individuals are selected
again according to the density estimation method; for the third case (lines 27–32), i.e., when the number of feasible solutions is more
than 𝑁, 𝑁 individuals are directly selected using the density estimation method and calculate the corresponding ﬁtness values.
    In addition, a population-auxiliary information migration strategy is also employed in this phase. The auxiliary population 𝑃1
selected by 𝐸𝑆2 can provide more objective information for the main population, while the main population 𝑃 selected by 𝐸𝑆1 can
provide more constraint information for the auxiliary population. The two populations generate oﬀspring through GCSO and perform
information interaction, respectively. Through the above strategies, the population enhances diversity while balancing objective
optimization and constraint satisfaction.

5.2. Ablation experiments

    In this section, we conduct a sensitivity analysis on the parameters 𝑁, 𝑟𝑎, and 𝜆𝑒 in ADCMEA. The population size 𝑁 aﬀects the
results to some extent. When the total number of evaluations is ﬁxed, a larger 𝑁 leads to fewer iterations. Conversely, a smaller 𝑁
for each generation leads to more iterations. In this experiment, the values of 𝑁 are set to 20, 60, 100, and 150; 𝑟𝑎 controls the ratio
between two stages of ADCMEA, with its values set to 0, 0.3, 0.6, and 1, where 0 and 1 represent only the constraint processing stage
and the objective optimization stage, respectively. 𝜆𝑒 represents the proportion of elite individuals, with its values set to 0.1, 0.4, 0.7,
and 1. Four types of welding paths were considered: straight lines, polygons, circular arcs, and L-shapes. The simulation parameters
for each path were set as described in Section 5.1. The average IGD for each constraint optimization problem was calculated using
Eq. (22).

5.3. Comparative experiments with peer constrained multiobjective evolutionary algorithms

    In recent years, many constrained multiobjective evolutionary algorithms have been proposed to solve practical optimization
problems. To verify the advantages of ADCMEA in solving the obstacle-avoidance inverse kinematics problem for redundant welding
manipulators, it is compared with 9 state-of-the-art constrained multiobjective evolutionary algorithms. The peer algorithms include
NSGAII [41], CMOEAD [43], BiCo [44], CCMO [37], IMTCMO [45], DAEAEO [38], CMOEMT [46], CMOES [47], and DDCMOEA
[48]. Each of the compared algorithms has its characteristics in balancing objectives and constraints. NSGAII, as a well-known
multiobjective optimization algorithm, proposed the nondominated sorting and crowding distance methods for selecting solutions. The
constraint handling technique uses the constraint dominance principle (CDP). CMOEAD extended decomposition-based multiobjective
evolutionary algorithms to solve constrained optimization problems. BiCo approximates the constrained Pareto front via the main
population and the archive population from two complementary directions, respectively. In CCMO, a co-evolutionary framework was
proposed to solve the primal constrained multiobjective optimization problem by introducing an auxiliary population. In IMTCMO, the
authors proposed an evolutionary multi-task constrained multiobjective evolutionary algorithm for solving test functions with high-
dimensional decision space constraints. DAEAEO adopted the idea of auxiliary population. In CMOEMT, a new two-stage algorithm
was presented to solve constrained multiobjective problems based on the proposed multitasking framework. In CMOES, the even
search strategy was deveploed to exploit and detect feasible regions. DDCMOEA used a dual population collaborative search method,
which can quickly ﬁnd constrained Pareto front. Therefore, selecting these algorithms as comparison algorithms is very representative,
and it can well represent the advantages of ADCMEA to solve the optimization problem in this paper.
    The parameters of each peer algorithm adopted the optimal values suggested in the literature, as shown in Table 3, where 𝐷
denotes the number of decision variables. For ADCMEA, the stage ratio is 𝑟𝑎 = 0.6, and the proportion of elite individuals is 𝜆𝑒 = 0.7.
For all algorithms on each welding scene, the maximum number of function evaluations 𝑚𝑎𝑥𝐹 𝐸 was set to 10,000, and the population
size is 𝑁 = 100.
    The experiments were performed on a Windows 10 i7-11800H 8-core CPU. The simulation platform is based on a combination of
CoppeliaSim software and MATLAB software. The speciﬁc implementation process is shown in Fig. 9. The function of CoppeliaSim
software is to visualize the welding process and transmit a position of each welding path point to MATLAB via a remote API. MATLAB
receives a position of each path point in real time and performs inverse kinematics calculation for each point. Inverse kinematics
calculation is performed using a constrained multiobjective evolutionary algorithm. One solution is selected from the ﬁnal Pareto so-
lution set as the ﬁnal joint conﬁguration. Priority is given to joint conﬁgurations that do not violate the obstacle-avoidance constraint.
The speciﬁc selection steps are as follows. If all solutions violate the obstacle-avoidance constraint, select a solution with the smallest
total constraint violation values among all solutions. If only one solution does not violate the obstacle-avoidance constraint, select the
current solution as the ﬁnal solution. If there are at least two solutions that do not violate the obstacle-avoidance constraint, select a
solution with the smallest overall constraint violation values among them. During the co-simulation, communication between the two
software programs is bidirectional. CoppeliaSim transmits a position to MATLAB, which then returns the ﬁnal joint angles back to
CoppeliaSim. Subsequently, the robotic arm sets its joints to position the welding torch tip at the target position. After completing one
communication cycle, CoppeliaSim resends a position to MATLAB. This process repeats cyclically until all path points are traversed.
    During the optimization process, each algorithm was run 10 times independently on the Platemo [49], and 8 cases were run in

5.5. Further discussions

    From the results in Tables 4–7 and Fig. 12, we can observe that the proposed ADCMEA demonstrates signiﬁcantly better perfor-
mance than other algorithms, which is attributed to its well-designed optimization mechanism. In ADCMEA, we introduce the GCSO.
Unlike the classical CSO, the GCSO also updates the winner individuals. This update mechanism enables the population to explore
more unknown positions, thereby enhancing its diversity. Furthermore, our developed ﬁtness evaluation strategy provides eﬃciency
in solution evaluation and selection, signiﬁcantly enhancing convergence. Beneﬁting from an eﬀective balance between diversity and
convergence, ADCMEA demonstrates satisfactory results in solving inverse kinematics problems.
    Regarding the handling of complex constraints, as shown in Fig. 12, ADCMEA satisﬁes the position error constraint but fails to
meet the orientation error constraint and joint continuity error constraint. The primary reasons are as follows. First, the constraint-
handling techniques inherent to the algorithm itself cannot adapt to the current complex constraints, and more advanced and eﬃcient

constraint-handling techniques need to be developed. Second, the constraint limits set in this paper–such as requiring orientation error
and joint continuity error not to exceed 0.01 rad–are overly strict. Thus, the feasible region formed by these constraints is extremely
small or nonexistent. Despite these limitations, our approach demonstrates satisfactory results in constraint handling. As shown in
Fig. 13, other algorithms cannot directly address joint continuity constraints. Solutions from these peer algorithms exhibit signiﬁcant
ﬂuctuations in joint angles, posing safety hazards during actual robotic welding operations. In contrast, the joint curves obtained by
ADCMEA are relatively smooth, making them highly suitable for practical applications.


6. Conclusion

    This paper reformulates the inverse kinematics problem of redundant robotic arms as a multiobjective optimization problem sub-
ject to constraints. The constraints encompass position error constraint, orientation error constraint, highly correlated joint continuity
error constraint, obstacle-avoidance constraint, etc. We modeled the obstacle-avoidance constraint using a data-driven prediction
method. The results demonstrate that, for all paths, the coeﬃcients of determination exceed 0.9. For the straight and polygonal
welding paths, 91.3 % of the samples exhibit a prediction error of less than 0.05 m, while for the circular and L-shaped welding
paths, 86.6 % of the samples show a prediction error of less than 0.05 m. The prediction accuracy satisfactorily fulﬁlls the modeling
requirements of the obstacle-avoidance constraints.
    To address this constrained multiobjective optimization problem, we proposed an auxiliary-based dual-stage constrained multiob-
jective evolutionary algorithm, referred to as ADCMEA. This algorithm divides the optimization process into an objective optimization
phase and a constraint handling phase, eﬀectively balancing objectives and constraints. In ADCMEA, we developed a global com-
petitive swarm optimizer and a novel ﬁtness evaluation strategy. Sensitivity analysis of ADCMEA parameters was also performed,
and the eﬀectiveness of ADCMEA was validated through comparisons with 9 state-of-the-art constrained multiobjective evolutionary
algorithms. The results demonstrated that ADCMEA yields markedly the best performance compared to peer algorithms on all welding
paths.
    Additionally, we conducted time eﬃciency analysis and robustness studies under data perturbations. Regarding computational
time, results indicate that the proposed ADCMEA achieves a time eﬃciency ranking in the middle of all algorithms. With respect to
sensitivity analysis under data perturbations, ADCMEA demonstrates strong robustness on the L-shaped path, polygonal path, and
straight path. However, for the circular path, performance and stability decline signiﬁcantly when the perturbation level increases
to 10 %. In the future, more eﬀective optimization strategies and constraint handling techniques can be proposed to further enhance
the performance, robustness, and time eﬃciency of ADCMEA.
